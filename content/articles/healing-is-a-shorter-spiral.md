faq1_q: Why does healing feel like going backwards?
faq1_a: Because the return of a familiar feeling is far more noticeable than the slow improvement around it. You remember the state, not the duration — so a two-hour dip can feel identical to the two-week version you had last year, and your brain files it as "nothing has changed" when the only honest comparison is how long it lasted.
faq2_q: What does non-linear healing actually mean?
faq2_a: That the shape is a downward trend with spikes, not a clean descent. Good weeks are followed by a bad Tuesday for no visible reason. Anniversaries, songs, smells and tiredness all reopen things briefly. Non-linear does not mean random — it means you have to zoom out past a single week to see the direction.
faq3_q: How do I know if I am actually making progress?
faq3_a: Three measures beat "do I feel better": how long the dip lasts, how fast you recognise what is happening, and what you do during it. If a spiral that used to cost five days now costs an evening, and you reach for a tool instead of a person or a bottle, that is progress even when the feeling is identical.
faq4_q: Is it normal to relapse emotionally after months of being fine?
faq4_a: Yes, and it is not a relapse in any meaningful sense. Memory and emotion are reconstructive — old material gets reactivated by cues you often cannot identify. One bad week after four good months is a spike in a downward trend, not a return to the start.

Four good months. Then one Tuesday you hear eleven seconds of a song in a pharmacy and you are flat on the floor of it again, and the first thought is not *this is hard* — it is:

**None of it worked.**

That sentence is the thing that does the damage. Not the Tuesday.

## Your brain compares states, not durations

Here is why the Tuesday lies to you.

When the old feeling returns, what comes with it is *recognition* — this exact texture, this exact weight. And because the state feels identical to the worst version of it, your brain reports back: we are where we were.

It does not report the data that actually matters:

- Last year this lasted **nine days**. This time it lasted **an evening**.
- Last year you did not know what was happening. This time you named it in twenty minutes.
- Last year it took you out of work. This time you made dinner anyway.

The state was the same. The spiral was a fraction of the size. But you only remember the state, because the state is what you felt — duration is something you have to deliberately measure.

::: science Why the old feeling can come back intact
Emotional memory is reconstructive, not archival. Material that has been processed can still be reactivated by a cue — a smell, a song, a date, plain exhaustion — and when it reactivates it can arrive with much of its original intensity. Reactivation is not regression. It means the memory exists, which it always will.
:::

## What progress actually looks like from the inside

Nothing. Honestly, nothing, for a while.

Real progress is unglamorous and almost entirely invisible from the inside:

- You still think about them, but you finish making the coffee while you do it.
- You still check, but you close the app without going three years deep.
- You still panic, but you wait an hour before sending anything.
- You still spiral, but you reach for the sheet by the bed instead of your phone.

None of that feels like healing. It feels like the same problem with slightly less drama. Which is exactly what healing is, before it becomes distance.

## The three honest measures

Stop asking *do I feel better?* It is the one question guaranteed to mislead you, because it samples one moment and extrapolates to your whole recovery.

Ask these instead.

### 1. Length
How long did it last this time, compared to the last time? Minutes, hours, days. Write the number down somewhere — you will not trust your memory of it later, and memory is precisely what distorts here.

### 2. Recognition speed
How fast did you know what was happening? *"Oh — this is the replay loop."* Last year it took three days to notice; this time twenty minutes. That gap is the skill, and it is the one that compounds.

### 3. What you did inside it
Did you use a tool, call someone, go outside, write the three lines? Or did you text them, drink it, scroll until 4am?

Behaviour inside the dip changes long before feelings do. It is also the only one of the three you directly control.

## The second arrow

There is the pain, and then there is what you do with the fact of the pain.

The song in the pharmacy was the first arrow: unavoidable, not your fault, over in eleven seconds.

The second arrow is the hour you then spend concluding that you are broken, that the work was wasted, that you will always be like this. That one is optional — not easily, not reliably, but it is the one with a handle.

Most of what people call "a bad week" is one bad hour plus six days of the second arrow.

::: try The one-line log
Keep a note with one line per dip: date, how long it lasted, what you did. That is all. In three months this note will be the only honest evidence you own, and on the bad Tuesday it will outvote your memory — which is the entire reason to keep it.
:::

## When the spiral is not shortening

Sometimes it genuinely is not getting shorter. Months in, the dips are as long and as deep, or the floor keeps dropping — and nothing in the toolkit touches it.

That is information, not failure. It usually means something underneath needs a different size of help: a professional who can see the whole picture, assess what is actually running, and work with it over time. Self-education is a good tool. It is not every tool, and knowing the difference is a skill too.

## The line to keep

You are not back at the start. You are at a familiar altitude on a much shorter rope.

The feeling returning is not evidence against your progress. How fast you came back is the evidence — and that is the number nobody taught you to count.
