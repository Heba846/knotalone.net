faq1_q: Why does a breakup feel physically painful?
faq1_a: Because romantic attachment runs partly on the same reward and motivation circuitry involved in wanting and seeking — and brain-imaging studies of recently rejected people show activity in regions tied to craving and to physical pain processing. Your body is not being dramatic. It is responding to the loss of something it had been treating as a resource.
faq2_q: How long does breakup withdrawal last?
faq2_a: The sharpest craving usually eases over weeks rather than days, and most people notice the intensity dropping in the first one to three months — but it is not linear, and grief over the shared future can continue long after the craving stops. If weeks two to six are worse than week one, that is the normal shape, not a relapse.
faq3_q: Why do I keep checking their social media?
faq3_a: Because each check re-doses the system you are trying to wean. Checking promises information and delivers a hit of the person instead, which resets the clock on the craving. This is also why no-contact works better than willpower: it removes the lever, rather than asking you not to pull it.
faq4_q: Is missing someone the same as still loving them?
faq4_a: Not necessarily. Withdrawal is attachment chemistry; it can keep firing for someone you have decided, with complete clarity, not to be with. "I miss them" and "I want to be with them" are two different sentences, and keeping them separate makes the first six weeks far less confusing.

Nobody warns you that it is physical.

You expect sadness. You expect to cry at a song. What you do not expect is to feel it in your chest and your hands, to forget to eat and then eat nothing but bread, to wake at 4am with the specific, bodily need to know what they are doing right now.

That part is not grief exactly. That part is withdrawal.

## Two pains, running at once

A breakup sets off at least two separate processes, and people fail at the first six weeks mostly because they treat them as one thing.

**1. Withdrawal.** Your brain had been running a motivational loop around one specific person — their messages, their presence, the small daily hits of them. Imaging studies of people who had recently been rejected found activity in reward and motivation regions associated with wanting and craving, and in areas involved in processing physical pain. The system is still looking for them. The reward stopped arriving.

**2. Grief.** Not for the person only — for the future. The flat you were going to rent. The version of yourself that existed in their company. The in-jokes that now have no second speaker.

Withdrawal is a craving and it responds to distance. Grief is a loss and it responds to being felt. If you try to grieve your way out of withdrawal you will keep re-dosing. If you try to white-knuckle your way through grief you will stay numb for a year.

::: science Why "it's only been two weeks" is bad maths
Weeks two to six are frequently worse than week one. The first days usually come with shock, logistics and adrenaline, which blunt the edges. When the adrenaline clears, the full absence arrives. Most people read that as failing at recovery. It is the shape recovery actually has.
:::

## Why checking their profile makes it worse

Checking feels like information-gathering. It is not. It is a dose.

You open the app looking for a feeling: relief that they are also miserable, or proof they have moved on so your brain can finally stop hoping. Either way, you get a small hit of the person — which tells the reward system they are still available to track.

Then the clock resets.

This is why no-contact beats willpower. Not as a punishment, and not as a strategy to make them miss you. It works because removing the lever is easier than refusing to pull it two hundred times a day.

## The questions that keep the loop alive

Certain questions are specifically unanswerable, and they are the exact ones your brain goes for:

- *Do they miss me?*
- *What would have happened if I had said less that night?*
- *Was any of it real?*

These questions feel like analysis. They function as contact — thinking about them is the closest available substitute for them.

Notice the shape: every one of them requires access to someone else's inner life. You will never get that access, which is precisely why the loop can run forever on nothing.

Trade them for questions that have answers. *What do I need tonight? What is one thing I outsourced to them that I now have to hold myself?*

## What actually helps in the first six weeks

### 1. Separate the two pains out loud

Say which one is running: *"This is craving"* or *"This is grief."*

Craving: distract, move, leave the house, let the wave pass — it is a wave, it peaks and drops.
Grief: stop, feel it, let it have the twenty minutes it is asking for.

Half of the exhaustion in week three is using the wrong tool on the wrong pain.

### 2. Write the no-send letter

Everything you would say if there were no consequences and no audience. No fairness, no editing, no hope of them reading it.

Then do not send it. The point is not communication — it is giving the unfinished conversation an ending that does not depend on their participation. This is the single most-used page in our Decompression Kit, and nobody expects it to work.

### 3. Rebuild one small thing that was outsourced

Long relationships quietly take over functions: who cooks, who plans Fridays, who you call when the test result comes.

Pick one. Take it back deliberately this week. Not as self-improvement — as repossession.

### 4. Measure the length, not the presence

You will not wake up one day not missing them. What happens is that the wave gets shorter. Forty minutes instead of a whole evening. An afternoon instead of three days.

Track that. It is the only progress metric that does not lie to you in week four.

::: try When the 3am craving hits
Do not open their profile. Write one line: "I want to check because I want to feel ___." Fill in the blank honestly — close, chosen, less alone, right about something. Then do one thing that serves that feeling and is not them. It will not be as good. That is how weaning works.
:::

## The part that is actually unfair

Withdrawal does not consult your judgement. You can know with total clarity that leaving was correct — that they lied, or were cold, or simply did not love you — and still crave them at 3am like a substance.

That contradiction is not confusion and it is not a sign you made a mistake. It is one system running on evidence and another running on chemistry, at different speeds.

The chemistry catches up. It just goes last.
