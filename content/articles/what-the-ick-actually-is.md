He ordered for you in a slightly performative voice. She said "yummy" about a sandwich. He ran for a bus and his bag bounced.

And something in you shut like a door.

Yesterday you liked him. Now you are cataloguing the way he says "no worries" and wondering how you will get through dinner. The switch had no warning and it does not respond to argument, which is the part people find frightening about it.

## Disgust is a fast system, not a considered one

The ick is a small experience of **disgust**, and disgust is unusual among feelings in how it operates. It is quick, it is physical, and it resists reasoning with — its job was to make you drop the bad food immediately, not to weigh the evidence.

So when disgust gets attached, even faintly, to a person, it arrives at full strength with no deliberation. There is no gradual cooling you could interrupt. There is a before and an after.

That is why "but he's lovely though" does nothing. You are not arguing with an opinion.

::: science Why it is so often a small, harmless thing
The trigger is rarely a real flaw. It is almost always a moment where someone looks briefly *unguarded* — clumsy, eager, trying. Those moments puncture an idealised image, and the gap between the image and the person registers as wrongness. The more idealised the image, the harder the puncture lands. This is also why the ick is far more common early, when the image is doing most of the work.
:::

## The two completely different things people call the ick

They look identical from inside. They are not the same.

### 1. Information arriving late

Sometimes you already knew. Something was off — the way he spoke to the waiter, a flicker of contempt, the fact that you are performing a version of yourself around her and are tired.

You did not let yourself know it, because knowing it would mean ending something that is, on paper, fine. So the knowledge waited and then came out sideways, attached to a sandwich.

**Tell by:** it keeps returning, with the same person, across weeks. It has company — a low dread before you see them, relief when plans cancel. And it is specific to *them*, not to the stage of the relationship.

### 2. A retreat, dressed as a preference

And sometimes the timing gives it away completely.

The ick arrives in the week after they said something warm. After they met your friends. After the first time you thought *oh, this could actually work.*

That is not a coincidence. Someone who could genuinely matter is also someone who could genuinely hurt you, and if your early evidence said closeness ends badly, the alarm fires on **availability**, not on behaviour. The ick is the first move of a retreat, and it is very good at disguising itself as taste, because taste requires no explanation and cannot be argued with.

**Tell by:** it arrived right after things got good. It happens with most people you start to like. The flaw is tiny and you know it is tiny. And underneath the disgust, if you sit still, there is something closer to fear.

## The question that separates them

Not *is this real?* — it feels real either way.

Ask: **what happened in the week before it?**

If the answer is "he was dismissive about my job twice" — that is information. Listen to it.

If the answer is "he said he'd met my family and really liked them, and I felt something drop" — that is a retreat. And it deserves a different response.

## What to do with each

**If it is information:** stop looking for a reason acceptable enough to say out loud. "I don't want to continue this" is a complete sentence. You do not need a flaw big enough to justify leaving; not wanting to is sufficient, and holding on until he does something bad enough to deserve it is worse for both of you.

**If it is a retreat:** do not act on it for two weeks, and say it out loud to one person who is not him. The ick fed by panic usually fades once the panic is named — often within days. The one fed by genuine incompatibility does not, and two weeks will tell you which you have.

Note what this is not: forcing yourself to stay with someone who repels you. It is refusing to end something on the strength of a bus-chase while you are in the middle of a protection response.

::: try The two-week rule
Write the trigger down, verbatim and undramatised: *he said "yummy".* Then write what happened in the seven days before it. Then do nothing for two weeks. If the feeling fades, it was fear. If it hardens, it was information — and now you can leave without needing a story that makes you the reasonable one.
:::

## One thing worth saying plainly

Somebody has had the ick about you.

Some version of you — eager, uncoordinated, trying — punctured someone's image, and a door closed, and they never told you why. Because there was no why. It was a sandwich.

That is worth knowing in both directions. It makes the feeling less of a verdict on anyone, including the person currently chasing a bus with their bag bouncing — who is, on the available evidence, just a person running for a bus.
