Somebody will tell you six months. Somebody else will tell you half the length of the relationship. Someone on the internet will say it took them two years and someone else will say three weeks and a haircut.

None of those are answers. Here is what can honestly be said.

## The honest version

**The acute phase** — the part with the physical texture, the 4am waking, the checking — usually eases within the first weeks to a couple of months. Studies following people through breakups generally find distress declining fairly steeply over the first months, with the steepest drop early once contact actually stops.

**Full recovery** — meaning the person becomes a part of your history rather than an open question — takes longer, and scales with four things:

- **How long it was.** More shared routine means more things to rebuild.
- **Whether you were left.** Being left tends to hurt longer, partly because it comes with unanswered questions, and unanswered questions keep the file open.
- **How much of your identity it held.** If your weekends, your plans and your idea of the next five years were joint, you are grieving a self as well as a person.
- **Whether contact has actually stopped.** This is the one that moves the number most, and the one most people quietly refuse to change.

So: weeks for the worst of it, months for most of it, around a year for a long and serious one — with enormous variation that says nothing about you.

::: careful About the "half the relationship length" rule
It is not from any study. It circulates because it sounds measurable. The harm is real: it either makes you panic for being behind, or convinces you to dismiss grief that has not finished. Delete it.
:::

## Why weeks two to six are usually the worst

Almost nobody is warned about this, so when it happens they conclude they are failing.

**Week one** comes with shock, adrenaline and logistics — who tells whom, moving the things, sorting the practicalities. Friends check in constantly. There is a strange energy to it. Some people even feel oddly fine.

**Weeks two to six** are when the scaffolding comes down. The adrenaline clears. The messages slow because everyone assumes the worst is over. And the absence stops being an event and becomes the ordinary condition of Tuesday.

That is not a relapse. That is the curve.

## Two pains, two different clocks

Running simultaneously, on different timetables:

**Withdrawal** is the physical one — restlessness, checking, appetite gone strange, the 3am craving for one specific person. Brain-imaging work with recently rejected people found activity in reward and motivation regions tied to wanting and craving, alongside areas involved in processing physical pain. It behaves like weaning, and it responds to distance.

**Grief** is the loss of the future — the flat you were going to rent, the in-jokes with no second speaker. It responds to being felt, and it does not care about distance.

Withdrawal usually fades first. Grief can resurface at an anniversary a year later for twenty minutes and then let you go again. Both are normal, and treating one with the other's medicine is most of why month two is so exhausting.

## The three measures that actually track progress

Stop asking *do I feel better*. It samples one moment and lies.

**Length.** How long did this dip last, against the last one? Nine days, then four, then an evening. Write the numbers down; your memory will not keep them honestly.

**Recognition speed.** How fast did you clock it? *Oh — this is the craving, not a sign.* Three days last month, twenty minutes now. That gap is the skill.

**Behaviour inside it.** Did you write the no-send letter, call someone, go outside? Or text them, check the profile, drink it? Behaviour changes well before feelings do, and it is the only one of the three you directly control.

## What reliably makes it longer

- **Checking their profile.** Each check re-doses the system you are weaning. It promises information and delivers a hit of them.
- **Staying "friends" immediately.** Sometimes right later. Almost never right now, while one of you is still hoping.
- **Hunting for closure from them.** Closure is nearly always something you write, not something they come back to hand you.
- **Keeping the shrine.** The hoodie, the playlist, the thread you reread. Box it, do not burn it — future you may want it, present you is being fed by it.

## What reliably makes it shorter

- **Actual no contact**, for a defined period, for your nervous system rather than as a tactic.
- **One rebuilt thing a week** that was outsourced to them — who you call with news, who plans Friday.
- **Naming which pain is running**, out loud: *this is craving* or *this is grief*. They need opposite responses.
- **The no-send letter.** Everything you would say if there were no consequences, written once, unedited, never sent.
- **Telling someone the embarrassing version.** The 2am draft, the profile checks. Shame about the behaviour usually lasts longer than the behaviour.

::: try The only tracking worth doing
One note, one line per dip: date, how long it lasted, what you did. Nothing else. In two months this note is the only honest evidence you own — and on the bad Tuesday it will outvote your memory, which is exactly when you need it to.
:::

## When it is not just a breakup

If, months on, you cannot work or sleep, feel hopeless rather than sad, or the grief has not shifted at all, that is worth taking to a professional. Not because you are failing at this — because a breakup can sit on top of something else, and that something else does not answer to a worksheet.

## The line to keep

You will not wake up one day not missing them. What happens is the wave gets shorter, and then one day you notice you did not check, and you cannot remember when you stopped.

That is the whole shape of it. Measure the length, not the presence.
