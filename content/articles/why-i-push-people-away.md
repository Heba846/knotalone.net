Three good weeks. Then they say something warm — *I've started really looking forward to these* — and something in you goes flat.

You reply shorter. You take longer. You find a flaw you did not notice before and turn it over like evidence. By the time they ask if everything's okay, you have constructed a reasonably convincing case for why this was never going to work.

And underneath all of it: you like them. That is the part that makes no sense.

## Closeness and exposure arrive together

Here is the thing nobody says plainly: being close to someone *is* being exposed to them. There is no version where one arrives without the other.

Every time you let someone matter, you hand them the specific ability to hurt you — not in theory, in practice. They learn what you are ashamed of. They become the person whose absence you would feel. The warmth and the vulnerability are the same object seen from two sides.

For some people that trade is unremarkable. For anyone whose early evidence said closeness is unreliable — someone who left, someone whose warmth depended on your behaviour, someone who was there and then abruptly was not — the nervous system drew a different conclusion. Not *closeness is good*. Closeness is **the beginning of a known sequence**.

So when the good feeling arrives, two things happen at once: you enjoy it, and a much older system registers a threat forming.

::: science Why the alarm is loudest with the good ones
This is not about how much you like someone in the abstract. It tracks stakes. A person you are indifferent to cannot do you any damage, so no protection fires. A person who could genuinely wreck you triggers the full system — which is why the pattern reliably ruins the promising ones and leaves the mediocre ones completely untouched.
:::

## What the pulling away is actually doing

Three jobs, all of them protective, none of them chosen:

**It restores control.** Being left is something that happens to you. Leaving is something you do. The second one hurts — but it hurts on your schedule, in a form you can brace for. A nervous system that has been blindsided before will take predictable pain over unpredictable pain almost every time.

**It gathers proof.** Going cold is a test, even when you would never call it one. If they chase, maybe they are real. If they drift, you were right, and being right is a thin consolation that still beats being surprised.

**It shrinks the drop.** The further you let yourself fall for someone, the further there is to fall. Cooling off pre-emptively feels like lowering yourself closer to the ground before anyone pushes.

Nothing on that list is manipulation. It is all defence, running without asking you first.

## The part that does the damage

It is not the withdrawal. It is that **the withdrawal is silent**.

From inside, you are managing a flood of something you cannot name. From outside, a person who was warm on Tuesday is clipped on Friday, with no explanation available. Humans fill informational gaps with the worst plausible story — so they conclude they did something wrong, or that you are losing interest, and they respond by either pursuing anxiously or backing off to protect themselves.

Either response confirms your forecast. Pursuit feels like pressure. Distance feels like proof.

That is the loop closing, and it closes without anyone behaving badly.

## What actually changes it

### 1. Narrate instead of act

This is the whole intervention, and it is embarrassingly short:

> "I've gone a bit quiet. It isn't about you — I do this when I start to actually care about someone. I'm telling you so you don't have to guess."

Ten seconds. It does not require you to stop feeling the pull to retreat; it only stops the retreat from being a mystery someone else has to interpret. Most of the harm in this pattern lives in the gap between what you are doing and what it looks like.

### 2. Catch it at the warm moment, not the cold one

By the time you are drafting the breakup text, the story is fully built and feels like analysis.

The intervention point is earlier — the moment right after something good. The nice message. The plan they made that assumed a future. Notice what your body does in that second: a tightening, a flatness, a sudden urge to be busy. That flinch is the beginning, and it is much easier to name than the elaborate case you will have constructed by Thursday.

### 3. Test the story before you act on it

Write the sentence your brain is insisting on. *They're getting bored. This is going to end badly. I can already see it.*

Then: what would you have to believe for that to be true, and what evidence from the last two weeks supports it?

Usually you find the "evidence" is one delayed reply and a feeling. That is not nothing — but it is also not a reason to end something, and seeing it written down in that size is often enough.

### 4. Stay ninety seconds longer

Not ninety minutes. Ninety seconds.

When you want to leave the conversation, close the laptop, go cold — stay a minute and a half past the urge. Your nervous system is predicting that closeness leads somewhere unbearable, and the only thing that updates a prediction is a repeated experience of it being wrong. Small, survivable doses. Ninety seconds at a time.

::: try This week
Pick one person you have been quietly cooling on. Send one unprotected sentence — no joke at the end, no exit built in. Something as plain as "I'm glad you're around." Then sit with how uncomfortable that is. The discomfort is the exercise.
:::

## The reframe that helps most

You are not afraid of intimacy. You are extremely good at surviving its loss, and that skill is currently running on a schedule nobody updated.

It worked once. It was probably the smartest thing available to you at the time. It is also why the last three good ones ended in a silence you built.

The opposite of pushing someone away is not clinging. It is staying, awkwardly, and saying so out loud.
