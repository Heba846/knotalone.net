faq1_q: What is negativity bias?
faq1_a: The tendency for negative events and information to affect you more strongly than equally intense positive ones. It shows up in attention, memory, learning and decision-making, and it is one of the most consistently replicated findings in psychology. It is a feature of normal brains, not a sign of pessimism.
faq2_q: Why do I remember criticism word for word?
faq2_a: Because it was encoded as useful threat data. Emotionally charged, self-relevant information gets deeper processing and stronger retrieval cues — and criticism is all three. Praise rarely needs to be acted on, so it is filed loosely, which is why it fades while the other sentence stays verbatim.
faq3_q: How do I stop dwelling on criticism?
faq3_a: Do not try to argue it away, which keeps it in working memory. Extract the actionable part in one sentence, decide what you will do with it, write it down, and deliberately re-encode the positive evidence by writing it in specific words. The bias is automatic; the correction has to be manual.
faq4_q: Is negativity bias the same as low self-esteem?
faq4_a: No. Negativity bias is universal. What differs is what you conclude from it: someone with harsh self-beliefs treats the criticism as confirmation of an existing verdict, so the sting lasts longer and generalises further. The bias is the same; the story it feeds is not.

Nine people said the piece was good. One said it was "fine, I guess."

It is now 11:40pm and you are not thinking about the nine.

You can even hear the sentence — the pause before "fine", the shrug implied. Meanwhile you could not reconstruct a single compliment verbatim if someone paid you.

This is not insecurity, or at least it is not only insecurity. It is a mechanism, and it is running in everyone you know.

## The asymmetry is real and it is measured

Across attention, memory, learning and decision-making, researchers keep finding the same thing: **bad is stronger than good**. Negative information is processed more thoroughly, remembered more durably and weighted more heavily than positive information of equal intensity.

It shows up everywhere once you look. Losses hurt more than equivalent gains please. Bad first impressions take far more evidence to overturn than good ones. People scan a page of feedback and land on the one critical line as if it were highlighted.

The reason is unglamorous. Historically, missing a threat cost more than missing an opportunity. A system that over-weights bad news survives; a balanced one does not get the chance to be balanced for long.

::: science Why the sentence stays verbatim
Criticism is emotionally charged, self-relevant and potentially actionable — three things that produce deeper encoding and stronger retrieval. Praise is pleasant and usually requires nothing of you, so it is filed casually. You are not failing to appreciate the compliments. They were stored in a cheaper format.
:::

## The three moves that make it worse

The bias is automatic. What happens next is not, and this is where the damage actually accumulates.

**Discounting the positive.** *They were being nice. They say that to everyone. They hadn't read it properly.* Every piece of contradicting evidence gets disqualified on arrival, which means the negative story never has to face anything. It stays undefeated because it is never challenged.

**Generalising.** "This paragraph is weak" becomes "I can't write." A specific, fixable observation gets promoted into an identity, and identities are not fixable — so there is nothing to do except feel bad.

**Rehearsing.** You replay the sentence, construct the perfect reply, imagine saying it. This feels like processing. It is not: nothing new is produced, and each rehearsal strengthens the memory you are trying to get rid of. You are not working through it. You are practising it.

## What actually helps

### 1. Separate the data from the verdict

Most criticism contains a small amount of usable information wrapped in a large amount of tone.

Write two lines:
- **What was actually said**, as plainly as possible: *the middle section lost them.*
- **What I turned it into**: *I'm not good at this and people are being polite.*

Seeing them side by side usually does most of the work. One of them is a note about a middle section; the other is a sentence about your whole life.

### 2. Decide the action, then close it

One question: is there something to do here?

If yes — write it down with a when. *Tighten the middle section on Saturday.* Written and scheduled, the loop has somewhere to go.

If no — say so explicitly, out loud: *there is nothing to do with this.* An unactionable loop runs forever precisely because it never gets closed. Naming it as closed is a real move, not a consolation prefix.

### 3. Re-encode the good evidence manually

The praise was stored badly. Store it again, on purpose.

Keep a file — notes app, paper, anywhere. When someone says something good, write it **verbatim**, with who said it and when. Not "people liked it". The actual words.

You will not want to read it on good days. Read it on the 11:40pm days, when your brain reports that nobody has ever said anything positive about your work. The file disagrees, in specific language, which is the only kind of disagreement that lands.

### 4. Count the sample, out loud

Nine to one. Say the ratio.

It will not feel true — the one still weighs more, because weight is what this mechanism manipulates. But stating the real proportion interrupts the implicit claim your brain is making: that the critical voice is the accurate one and the rest were being kind.

### 5. Notice who you do this to

The same asymmetry runs in the other direction. The one sharp thing you said to a friend is probably the thing they still carry, and your nine warm ones evaporated.

Which is a reason to be specific with praise — vague compliments do not stick, concrete ones occasionally do — and to be careful where you are sharp.

::: try Tonight
Write the critical sentence down, exactly. Underneath it write the one thing you will actually change, or the words "nothing to change here". Then close the notebook. That is the whole intervention, and it works because it converts an open loop into a finished one.
:::

## The reframe

Your brain is not broken and it is not pessimistic. It is running a threat-detection system that is deliberately miscalibrated in the safe direction, because the cost of the two kinds of error was never symmetrical.

What is in your hands is not the weighting. It is what you do in the twenty minutes after the sentence lands — extract, decide, close, and deliberately re-store the evidence that got filed too cheaply.

Nine to one. Say it out loud. It is still true at 11:40pm.
