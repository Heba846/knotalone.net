"Five more minutes" was forty.

"I'll leave at seven" became 7:35, with no identifiable moment where the extra thirty-five minutes went.

"I'll start it tomorrow" was eleven days ago, and tomorrow still feels like a place you are going to get to.

If you have been told this is disorganisation, or not caring, or needing to try harder, none of those explanations fit the evidence — because they would predict that you are late to everything, and you are not. You are often early to things you are anxious about. The pattern is weirder than carelessness.

## Two settings: now, and not now

The most useful description of time blindness is also the bluntest: time comes in two states.

There is **now** — vivid, demanding, real. And there is **not now** — a flat grey space containing everything from twenty minutes away to next March, all at roughly the same distance.

A deadline on Friday is not-now on Tuesday. It is not a lesser emergency than Thursday's version; it is a different category of thing. Then at some point on Thursday it crosses into now, and the full force of it arrives at once — which is why the all-nighter is possible and the three calm weeks before it were not.

Most time-management advice assumes you can feel next Friday from here. That is precisely the sense that is unreliable.

::: science What is actually different
Time perception leans on the same executive machinery as working memory and self-regulation, which is exactly where ADHD shows its differences. Studies of time estimation and reproduction fairly consistently find people with ADHD less accurate — both over- and under-estimating, with the error growing as intervals get longer. The sense is noisy, not absent. And a noisy signal is worth less than no signal, because you still trust it.
:::

## The four ways it shows up

**Hyperfocus eats hours.** Deep in something interesting, the clock simply stops reporting. You look up and it is dark. This is the same mechanism as the deadline problem, inverted: when *now* is maximally engaging, the passage of time gets no attention at all.

**Transitions take a tax.** "I'll come in a second" is sincere. The problem is not the willingness to stop — it is that stopping requires noticing that enough time has passed, and that notification never arrives.

**Estimates are fiction.** Everyone underestimates tasks; it is one of the most reliable findings in planning research. Add an unreliable internal clock and the error compounds, which is how a "quick" errand eats an afternoon.

**Waiting mode.** A dentist appointment at 3pm destroys the entire day, because something that must be attended to later sits in the *now* category and refuses to move. The hours before it cannot be used for anything, and from outside this looks like procrastination.

## What actually helps

The principle is one line: **stop trying to feel time and start seeing it.**

### 1. Make time physically visible

A digital clock tells you what time it is. It does not show you how much is left, which is the thing you cannot sense.

An analogue clock face does. A visual countdown timer — the kind where a coloured wedge shrinks — does it better. Sand timers work for short tasks for the same reason. The point is converting a quantity you cannot feel into a shape you can see from across the room.

### 2. Set the alarm for the start, not the end

Most people set a reminder for when the thing happens. That is far too late — you still have to notice the gap, estimate the preparation, and begin.

Set it for the moment you must *begin*: not "dinner at 7", but "start cooking, 6:15". Better still, two alarms: one at the start, one ten minutes before as a warning that the state you are in is about to need to change.

### 3. Keep an estimate-versus-actual log, for one week

Write down how long you think a task will take. Then write what it actually took. Do not improve anything yet, just collect.

After a week you will have a personal multiplier — most people land somewhere between 1.5x and 2.5x. You do not have to learn to estimate accurately. You have to learn your own correction factor and then apply it like a conversion rate.

### 4. Build buffer in by default

If the journey takes forty minutes, the plan is an hour. Not because you are slow — because the forty minutes does not include finding keys, the thing you remember at the door, or the task you start while "waiting".

A reliable rule beats an accurate one here. Accuracy requires judgement every time; a flat buffer requires none.

### 5. Externalise the future

A deadline that exists only in your calendar app is not-now until the morning it arrives. Put it where your eyes already go: a wall, a whiteboard, a card on the desk. Count down in days, physically.

This is why so many ADHD systems look childish. They are not childish — they are external, and external is the whole trick.

::: try Today, one thing
Set a visible timer for your next task — a kitchen timer, a phone timer face-up, anything with a shrinking shape. Not to hurry you. Just to find out what thirty minutes actually feels like, once, with your eyes instead of your gut.
:::

## The thing to stop doing

Stop building plans that require you to notice time passing.

Every system you abandoned assumed an internal clock you do not reliably have. The ones that stick put the clock outside your head: on the wall, in the alarm, on the card, in the written number. That is not a workaround for a deficiency. It is the correct design for the equipment.

And when you miss something anyway — and you will — the useful question is not *why am I like this*. It is: **where could the time have been visible, and wasn't?**

That question has answers. The other one does not.
