Someone walks into you in a shop and you say sorry.

The restaurant brings the wrong order and you apologise for mentioning it. You say sorry before asking a question, sorry for taking up time, sorry for crying, sorry for being sad on a Tuesday. You have apologised for apologising too much — probably this week.

The usual explanations are "politeness" and "low self-esteem." Neither really fits, because you are not actually sorry, and you do not believe you have done anything wrong. The word arrives before any of that gets consulted.

## It is a reflex that works

Here is what an apology does in a room, within about a second: it lowers the temperature, it hands the other person the higher position, and it pre-empts any anger that might have been coming.

That is extremely useful if you learned early that tension in a room could turn into something worse — shouting, a long silence, a withdrawal of warmth, a parent you then had to manage. In that environment, apologising first is not weakness. It is the fastest available way to make a room safe.

This pattern often gets called **fawning** or appeasement: not fight, not flight, but *make them not angry.* It is a widely described response and a very effective one. The problem is not that it failed. It is that it worked so reliably it never got updated.

::: science Why it survives into safe rooms
Safety behaviours are self-maintaining. You apologise, the moment passes smoothly, and your brain records: *the apology prevented something.* It never gets to find out that nothing was coming. So the strategy is confirmed every single time, including — especially — in the hundreds of rooms where it was never needed.
:::

## What it quietly costs

Not drama. Something slower.

**It files you as the one at fault.** Say sorry enough and people absorb the framing. Over years, groups quietly organise around who is the easy one, and the easy one gets the inconvenient end of most arrangements.

**It makes your real apologies worth less.** If "sorry" arrives forty times a day, the one that matters cannot be distinguished from the chair you bumped.

**It costs you the request.** "Sorry, this is probably stupid, but could I maybe —" buries the ask under so much pre-emptive defence that people answer the apology instead of the question.

**It teaches you about yourself.** This is the real cost. Say *sorry for existing* in a hundred small ways and some part of you starts treating your presence as something that requires an excuse.

## The swap, not the ban

Trying to simply stop fails, because the reflex is doing a job and removing it leaves the job undone — which is why "stop apologising" makes people more anxious, not less.

Give it somewhere to go. **Gratitude does the same social work and files you differently.**

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| Sorry I'm late | Thanks for waiting |
| Sorry for going on about this | Thanks for listening |
| Sorry, can I ask something | Can I ask something |
| Sorry for the delay | Thanks for your patience |
| Sorry I'm such a mess | Thanks for being here with me |
| Sorry, is this okay? | Does this work for you? |

Same warmth. Same acknowledgement that the other person gave you something. No confession attached.

## The pause, and the sixty seconds

Catch it once a day. One *sorry* you were about to say, held for three seconds. Then say the real sentence underneath it — or nothing at all.

What follows is a few seconds of unresolved tension, which is exactly the thing the apology existed to prevent. It will feel wrong. That discomfort peaks and fades in under a minute, and that minute is the whole exercise: your system finding out, experimentally, that nothing happens.

Nothing happens. Nobody is angry. The moment passes. You just never let it get far enough to find out.

## Keep the real ones

None of this is an argument for never apologising. A specific, unhedged apology is one of the most valuable things a person can say.

> "I was short with you yesterday and it wasn't fair. I'm sorry."

That one has a subject and no padding. It costs something, which is why it lands. The reflex version costs nothing, which is why it does nothing.

The goal is fewer apologies that mean more — not a harder person.

::: try This week
Count them. One day, just notice, no changing. Most people are shocked by the number and that is the point — you cannot interrupt something you cannot see. Then pick one recurring version and swap it for the thank-you equivalent. One. That is enough.
:::

## The reframe

You are not too polite, and you are not broken. You learned, young and well, how to make a room safe by taking up slightly less space in it.

That skill is real and it made you easy to be around. It is also why people underestimate you, and why you are tired in a way that is hard to explain.

You are allowed to be in the room. You do not need to apologise for the shape you take up in it.
