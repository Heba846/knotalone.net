faq1_q: Why doesn't communicating fix my relationship?
faq1_a: Because talking more is not the same as asking clearly. Hinting, testing and arguing about who is right all count as communication and none of them produces an actionable request. The useful change is usually fewer words, aimed at one specific need.
faq2_q: How do I ask for what I need without sounding needy?

"You two just need to communicate."

You have been communicating for eleven months. There was the four-paragraph message at 1am. There was the conversation in the car. There was the one that started at 11pm and ended at 2:40am and solved nothing.

More talking has not worked, so either the advice is wrong or you are broken.

The advice is wrong. Or rather: it names a destination and calls it a direction.

## Most "communicating" is three other things

### Hinting

> "It's fine." *(said flatly)*
> "Must be nice to have that much free time."
> Going quiet so they notice and ask.

Hinting exists for one reason: **it cannot be refused.** If you never state it plainly, nobody can say no plainly, and a vague request can never be turned down.

The cost is that nobody can say yes either. And they are now interpreting a mood instead of answering a question — usually incorrectly, and usually defensively.

### Testing

> Not texting first, to see how long they take.
> Saying you might cancel, to see if they object.
> "Do you even want me here?"

A test is a request with its face covered. It feels safer, because the need is never exposed.

But from the other side it is a trap with no visible rules. Pass or fail, they do not know what the exam was — and if they fail one they did not know they were sitting, you get your evidence and they get an accusation.

### Litigating

> "That's not what happened."
> "I only did that because you—"
> Scrolling back to find the message that proves it.

This is the 2am conversation. It feels like communication; it is a trial. Someone has to be wrong at the end, so both people defend — and nobody defending is listening.

**None of these three gives the other person something to do.** That is the test for whether talking was communication.

::: science Why the shape matters more than the sincerity
A complaint describes the past and characterises a person: *you never make time for me.* It gets defended, because it is an accusation about identity. A request describes the future and names an action: *can we keep Thursday evenings?* It gets answered — yes, no, or a counter-offer. All three of those are usable. "No" is usable. A defence is not.
:::

## The actual skill: one need, one sentence

> "I need to hear that we're okay. Can you tell me?"
> "When you went quiet at dinner I got scared. What was going on?"
> "I'd like one evening a week that we don't give away."

Notice what is missing: the preamble, the apology, the evidence, the history, the pre-emptive defence.

Those get added because you are trying to make the request **refusal-proof** — build the case well enough and they cannot say no. But the longer the case, the more it reads as a charge, and people answer charges instead of requests.

Short requests are easy to meet. Long ones sound like court.

## Why the short version is so hard

Because a plain request can be plainly refused, and the refusal would be about something you actually admitted wanting.

Hinting protects you from that. If they fail to notice, it was never a real ask — you never risked anything. The hinting is not a communication failure. It is a working piece of protection, doing exactly what it was built to do.

Which is why "just be direct" lands as badly as "just communicate". It is asking you to give up a shield on the strength of good advice.

The way through is not courage, it is **size**. Make the first request small enough that a no would be survivable. *"Can we have dinner Thursday?"* Not *"do you still love me?"* Build evidence with small asks before you risk the large one.

## When they are the one who will not talk

Sometimes you are being clear and still getting nothing.

Try naming the pattern rather than the person: *"I notice I ask and you go quiet, and then I ask louder. Can we do that differently?"* Patterns are easier to agree about than blame.

Ask for a time instead of an answer: *"Not now then — when?"* For someone who shuts down under pressure, a scheduled conversation is a far smaller ask than an immediate one. And someone who takes the time and comes back is not avoiding you; someone who never comes back is answering you.

If the answer is consistently nothing — no time, no conversation, no change, over months — then you have your information. Not every silence is a communication problem. Some of them are the message.

::: try The one-sentence version
Take the thing you have been hinting about for two weeks. Write it as one sentence: a need, an action, no preamble. Read it back. If there is an apology, a history or a justification in there, cut it. Then send the sentence that is left.
:::

## The honest summary

"Just communicate" is not wrong, it is empty — like telling someone to "just be healthy".

The skill underneath it is small and specific: **say one need, in one sentence, to someone who can act on it, and let them answer.**

That is harder than four paragraphs at 1am. It is also the only version that has ever worked.
