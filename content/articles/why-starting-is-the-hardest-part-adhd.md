faq1_q: Why can I do hard tasks but not easy ones with ADHD?
faq1_a: Because difficulty is not the variable your activation system is reading. Interest, novelty, challenge and urgency are. A hard task that is interesting or urgent supplies all the signal you need; a two-minute task that is dull and deadline-free supplies none — so it sits on the list for a month while you do something objectively harder.
faq2_q: Is task paralysis a real thing?
faq2_a: Yes, though it is a descriptive term rather than a clinical diagnosis. It describes the state where a task is known, wanted and still cannot be initiated — the gap between intention and action. It is extremely common in ADHD and it responds much better to shrinking the first step than to trying harder.
faq3_q: How do I start a task when I physically cannot?
faq3_a: Shrink the first step until it is almost insulting — open the document, write the subject line, put one shoe on. The wall is at initiation, not at execution, so the goal is to get past initiation rather than to finish. Pair it with a body double (anyone present, doing their own thing) and an external timer.
faq4_q: Why do deadlines suddenly make everything possible?
faq4_a: Because urgency is one of the few signals the activation system reliably responds to. A deadline manufactures that signal, which is why the night before works and the three calm weeks before it did not. The problem is that it only works at the last minute and it taxes you heavily — so the practical move is to create smaller, earlier, artificial deadlines.

You wrote the difficult thing in one sitting. Four hours, no breaks, forgot to drink water, produced something genuinely good.

The email that says *"yes, Thursday works"* has been on your list since the 4th.

Every explanation you have been handed for this is about character: lazy, inconsistent, not applying yourself, could do it if you really wanted to. None of them fit the evidence. Lazy people do not accidentally work for four hours.

## Importance is not a signal your brain can read

Here is the uncomfortable mechanism: the system that gets you *into* a task does not respond to how important the task is.

It responds to interest, novelty, challenge and urgency. If a task carries one of those, activation happens almost without you. If it carries none, you can know the task matters, want to do it, feel guilty about not doing it — and still not start, because "important" is not an input it accepts.

This is why the usual advice fails so specifically. "Just prioritise" assumes importance drives action. For a lot of people it does. If it drove yours, you would have sent the email on the 4th.

::: science Starting and doing are two different jobs
It helps to separate initiation from execution. Execution is often fine, sometimes excellent — the four-hour sprint is real. The failure is at initiation: the handover from intending to moving. Nearly all useful ADHD strategy is about reducing the cost of that handover rather than increasing effort once you are moving.
:::

## Why small tasks are often the worst

A one-line email is dull, has no deadline, and takes two minutes. Zero interest, zero urgency, zero challenge — no signal at all.

Worse, it never feels worth planning for, so it gets re-postponed daily. And every repost adds a layer: the original task, plus the shame of it still being there, plus the mystery of why something this small defeats you.

By day eleven you are not avoiding an email. You are avoiding what the email now means about you.

## The reload fee you have been paying all day

There is a second cost nobody bills you for.

Every time you switch what you are doing — a notification, a question, a half-remembered errand — your brain unloads one set of rules and loads another. That reload is not free. In lab tasks, people are measurably slower and more error-prone right after a switch than when they stay on one rule. It is called switch cost, and it applies to everyone.

Now picture a day made almost entirely of switches. The fatigue at 6pm is not from low effort. It is the fee, charged a few hundred times. (You can feel it yourself in about ninety seconds in [the switch cost experiment](/games/switch-cost/) — round two is the honest one.)

## What actually works

### 1. Shrink the first step until it is almost insulting

Not "write the report". *Open the document and type a bad title.*

Not "reply to the email". *Write the first four words.*

The wall is at initiation. Once moving, you tend to keep going — which means the entire game is making the first step small enough that it does not need activation energy you do not have.

### 2. Decide in advance where and when

Vague intentions lose to specific ones. The research on implementation intentions is consistent: formulating "when situation X happens, I will do Y" improves follow-through compared to a general goal.

*"I'll do it tomorrow"* → nothing.
*"When I sit down with coffee at 9, I write the subject line"* → something.

You are pre-making the decision so that the moment does not require a fresh one.

### 3. Manufacture urgency earlier and smaller

If urgency is one of the few signals that works, stop waiting for the real deadline to supply it.

A 15-minute timer. A friend expecting a screenshot at 2pm. A body double on a video call doing their own work. These are not productivity theatre — they are external supply of the signal your system needs.

### 4. Batch like with like

Every rule change costs the fee. So do emails in one block, calls in another, admin in a third.

The win is not tidiness. It is paying the reload once instead of forty times.

### 5. Make time physical

Time blindness is not a figure of speech — the sense of *how long that took* and *how long until that* can be genuinely unreliable. Abstract time will not hold you.

Use an analogue timer where you can see the shape of the hour. Put the deadline on a wall, not in an app that needs opening. Make time something you can look at.

::: try The two-minute entry
Pick the oldest thing on your list — the dumb small one. Set a timer for two minutes and do only the first physical action: open the tab, write the greeting, find the file. When the timer goes you are allowed to stop. Most of the time you will not, and either way you have beaten initiation, which was the actual boss.
:::

## The one thing to stop doing

Stop buying systems that assume you will become a different person by Tuesday.

The planner you abandoned in nine days did not fail because you lack discipline. It failed because it was designed for a brain that runs on importance, and yours runs on interest and urgency. A system that fights your wiring needs your willpower to stay alive — and willpower is exactly the resource that is unreliable here.

Design for the brain you have. It is a much better brain than the story you have been told about it.
