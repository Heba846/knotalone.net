faq1_q: Why can't I stop thinking at night?
faq1_a: Because the loop lost its competition, not because it got stronger. During the day, other demands interrupt it constantly. In a dark, quiet room with nothing to do, your attention has no alternative task — so the thought you have been postponing since the afternoon finally gets the whole stage.
faq2_q: Does trying to clear your mind work?
faq2_a: Usually the opposite. Actively trying to suppress a thought tends to increase how often it comes back — the harder you push, the more monitoring you do, and monitoring keeps it live. Redirecting attention onto something mildly absorbing works much better than attempting to think about nothing.
faq3_q: Should I get out of bed if I can't sleep?
faq3_a: Yes, if you have been lying awake roughly twenty minutes or more. Staying in bed awake and frustrated teaches your brain that bed is where thinking happens. Get up, go somewhere dim and boring, do something undemanding, and come back when you feel sleepy. This is a standard part of insomnia treatment and it is more effective than lying there trying harder.
faq4_q: What is a worry window?
faq4_a: A fixed ten to fifteen minutes earlier in the day, at the same time daily, when you deliberately write out everything you are worried about. It gives the loop a guaranteed slot, so when it arrives at midnight you can reschedule it rather than fight it. It is used in worry and insomnia treatment because a bid for attention that has a future appointment stops fighting for this one.

It is 1am. You have an early start. You have been lying here long enough that the time itself is now one of the things you are anxious about.

Everything you have been told to do at this moment — clear your mind, count breaths, think positive — is either useless or actively making it louder.

Here is what works on the actual mechanism, in the order I would use it.

## 1. Give the loop an appointment (before 6pm)

The single highest-leverage move happens hours before bed.

Pick ten to fifteen minutes, same time every day — early evening works well. That is your **worry window**. You sit with paper and write out everything on the pile: the conversation, the email, the test result, the money. No solving required. No tidying.

Then, at 1am, when it shows up, you are not fighting it. You are rescheduling it: *not now — 6pm tomorrow, like always.*

It sounds far too simple. It is standard practice in worry and insomnia treatment for one reason: a loop that is bidding for attention will accept a guaranteed future slot. One that has no outlet at all has to fight for every slot, and 1am is when it finally wins.

## 2. Write three lines, not three pages

If it is already 1am and no window happened, do the compressed version. On your phone, in the dark, no editing:

1. **The scene** — one sentence. *"He said he needed space."*
2. **The verdict** — what you are afraid it proves. *"I am too much and people leave."*
3. **One fact against it** — something that actually happened this month. *"Dina called me first when her dad got sick."*

Three lines. Then phone face down.

Putting feelings into specific words takes heat out of them — vague distress is far harder for your brain to put down than a named fear. And the counter-fact matters because the loop is a court case with only a prosecutor; one piece of defence evidence changes the proceedings more than another hour of argument.

## 3. Stop trying not to think

Thought suppression tends to backfire: in classic experiments, people told not to think of something thought of it more, both while suppressing and afterwards. Pushing a thought away requires checking whether it has gone — and the checking keeps it alive.

So do not aim for an empty mind. Aim for an **occupied** one.

Give attention something mildly absorbing and non-threatening: mentally walk a familiar route in detail, name every object in a room from memory, recount the plot of a film you love in order. Boring, sensory, structured. It is not a trick to avoid your feelings; it is handing your attention a different job so it stops reaching for the only one available.

## 4. The twenty-minute bed rule

If you have been awake roughly twenty minutes or more: **get up.**

Not onto your phone, not into bright light. Somewhere dim and dull — a chair, the kitchen table. Read something undemanding. Come back when you actually feel sleepy.

Lying in bed awake, frustrated, for two hours teaches your brain an association: *bed is where we lie awake and think.* Breaking that association is one of the most reliable parts of insomnia treatment, and it is the opposite of what willpower tells you to do.

Yes, you will be more tired tomorrow. You are trading one night to stop building a habit that costs you a hundred.

## 5. Close the open loops externally

A surprising share of 1am noise is not emotional at all. It is unfinished admin that your brain is holding because it does not trust you to remember: the renewal, the message, the thing you promised your mother.

Open loops keep a claim on attention until they are closed or externalised. So externalise them. One running note, by the bed, dumped without priorities or order.

Your brain will let go of what it believes something else is holding. It will not let go of what it is holding alone.

## 6. Decide the next action, not the outcome

Overthinking persists where there is nothing to decide.

*Will he leave me?* — undecidable, runs forever.
*Will I text him tomorrow, and what will it say?* — decidable, and once decided it closes.

If a worry has an action, name the action and the time: *"Tomorrow at 10 I will send: are we okay?"* If it has no action — and many do not — say that out loud too: *"There is nothing to do about this tonight."* Naming a loop as actionless is a form of closing it, which is why it quiets faster than arguing with it.

## 7. Make tomorrow's version shorter

Three things cut the next night's loop before it starts:

- **A hard stop on input.** Scrolling until lights-out hands your brain a fresh stack of unprocessed material at the exact moment it has nothing else to chew.
- **Any daylight and any movement.** Both help sleep pressure, and neither needs to be a programme.
- **One thing written down for the morning.** The anchor task. Deciding it tonight stops it being rehearsed all night.

## When it is not a loop

Chronic insomnia, panic at night, nightmares after trauma, or lying awake with hopelessness are different problems than a busy head, and they respond to different help — including treatments that work well, like CBT for insomnia, which is more effective long-term than most people expect.

If most nights look like this, that is worth taking to a professional. Not because you failed at the seven things. Because the right tool exists and it is bigger than a worksheet.

---

**The short version:** give the loop a slot earlier, write three lines instead of lying there arguing, occupy your attention rather than emptying it, and get out of bed if twenty minutes pass. You are not trying to win against your brain tonight. You are closing a file so it stops reopening.
