faq1_q: Why does overthinking get worse at night?
faq1_a: Because the distractions are gone and the loop no longer has competition. During the day, work, noise and other people keep interrupting the replay. In bed there is nothing else for attention to do, so the loop gets the whole stage — which is why it feels like it arrived at night, when really it was waiting all day.
faq2_q: Is replaying conversations a sign of anxiety?
faq2_a: It can be part of an anxiety picture, and it can also happen to people with no anxiety disorder at all. On its own it is a common cognitive habit, not a diagnosis. What matters is how long it runs, how much it costs you, and whether it is stopping you from sleeping or living — that is a conversation for a professional, not a quiz.
faq3_q: How do I stop replaying a conversation?
faq3_a: Give the loop the ending it is hunting for. Write the scene in one sentence, write the verdict you think it proves about you, then write one fact from the last month that argues against that verdict. Naming it in specific words lowers the intensity; the counter-fact stops the loop from running unopposed.
faq4_q: What is the difference between reflecting and ruminating?
faq4_a: Reflection produces something — a decision, a sentence you will say, a thing you will do differently. Rumination produces another lap. If you have thought about it nine times and have no new output, you are not analysing it any more; you are rehearsing it.

You said the thing. Or you did not say the thing. Either way it is 2am and your brain is running the scene again, from a slightly different angle, with better lighting, as if this take will be the one that resolves it.

It will not resolve it. And that is not because you are bad at thinking.

## The loop is an open file, not a flaw

Your brain is unusually bad at leaving things unfinished. An interrupted task keeps a low-level claim on attention until it gets closed — the effect psychologists have been circling since Bluma Zeigarnik noticed waiters remembered unpaid orders better than paid ones. The claim is useful. It is why you remember to go back for the laundry.

A conversation that ended badly, or ambiguously, or with someone going quiet, is exactly that kind of open file. Something socially important happened and your brain cannot file it, because it does not know the outcome. Are we okay? Did I lose standing? Is this person leaving?

So it reopens the file. Each replay is an attempt to extract an ending from footage that does not contain one.

::: science Why it feels productive
Replaying is effortful, and effort usually correlates with progress — so the loop borrows the feeling of work. That is the trap. Rumination produces no new output: no decision, no sentence you will actually say, no changed behaviour. Reflection ends. Rumination laps.
:::

## Why the loop is louder at night

Nothing about the loop changes at night. Its competition does.

All day, your attention is being taken: work, messages, traffic, someone asking where the charger is. The replay gets interrupted constantly. Then you lie down in a dark, quiet room with no task and no input, and the only thing left on the schedule is the thing you have been avoiding since 4pm.

This is also why "just go to sleep" fails. You are not lying there because you forgot to be tired. You are lying there because the file is open and the room finally went quiet enough to hear it.

## The verdict underneath

Here is the part people miss: the loop is almost never about the scene.

Replay the sentence that haunts you and listen for what it supposedly proves. There is usually a verdict in there.

- *"He said he needed space"* → **I am too much.**
- *"She did not reply"* → **I do not matter to her.**
- *"I went blank in the meeting"* → **I am not good enough for this job.**

The scene is just evidence being filed in support of a case. That is why winning the argument in your head never helps — your brain is not litigating the conversation, it is litigating you.

Which means the way out is not a better version of the scene. It is a direct answer to the verdict.

## What actually interrupts it

### 1. Put it in specific words

Not "I feel awful". Specific: *"I am scared that going quiet is how he tells me he is done."*

Putting feelings into precise language reliably takes some heat out of them — the research literature calls it affect labelling, and it shows up in both self-report and brain-imaging work: naming the feeling moves the work out of pure alarm and into language. It does not delete the feeling. It stops it running the room.

### 2. Write the verdict, then one fact against it

One sentence for the scene. One for the verdict. One fact from the last month that contradicts the verdict.

Not a pep talk — a fact. *"My friend called me first when her dad got sick."* Facts are what your brain skipped while it was building the case.

### 3. Give it a worry window

Pick ten minutes, earlier in the day, same time daily. That is when the loop is allowed to run, on paper, with full permission.

When it shows up at 2am, you are not fighting it, you are rescheduling it: *not now, 6pm tomorrow.* This sounds too simple to work. It is a long-standing technique in insomnia and worry treatment, and it works because the loop is partly a bid for attention — and a bid that has a guaranteed future slot stops needing to fight for this one.

### 4. Close it externally

Write the open thing down somewhere you trust. A note, a list, a sticky on the laptop.

An open loop that has been written down stops needing to be held. This is also why the planner people keep abandoning works for exactly nine days: it is not discipline, it is externalising. Your brain will let go of what it believes something else is holding.

::: try Tonight, when it starts
Three lines, on your phone, in the dark. The scene. The verdict. One fact against it. Then put the phone face down. You are not trying to feel better — you are closing the file so your brain stops reopening it.
:::

## When it is more than a loop

Some replaying is normal. Replaying that keeps you up most nights, follows a trauma, fuels panic, or comes with hopelessness is worth taking to a professional who can actually meet you — not because something is wrong with you, but because the loop may be fuelled by something a worksheet cannot reach.

That is not a failure of self-help. It is just the right size of tool for the job.

## The one line to keep

You are not overthinking because you are weak or dramatic. You are running a mechanism that evolved to stop you losing your place in a group, and it does not know the conversation is over.

Give it the ending. That is the whole move.
