Your manager says "thanks, got it."

Not "great work." Not a single exclamation mark. Just *thanks, got it.*

And now there is something hot in your chest, you are rereading the message for the fourth time, you are drafting an apologetic follow-up, and some part of you has already concluded you are about to be managed out.

Nothing happened. That is what makes it so hard to explain to anyone.

## This is a threat system, not a self-esteem problem

People hear "sensitive to rejection" and picture someone fragile, or vain, or needing constant praise. That is not the shape of it.

It behaves like an **alarm**. It is fast — ahead of thought. It is physical — heat, a drop, nausea. It is specific to one channel: signs of disapproval from other people. And it fires on **ambiguity** rather than on actual rejection.

That last part is the key. A clear "no" is often easier to take than a short reply, because a clear no contains information. A neutral face contains none, and a threat-tuned system resolves missing information toward danger — historically, a false alarm cost far less than a missed one.

So you are not reading too much into it. You are reading *something* into a signal that carried nothing, which is what the system was built to do.

::: science On the term RSD
"Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is widely used in ADHD communities and by many clinicians, but it is not a formal diagnosis and does not appear in the diagnostic manuals. Rejection sensitivity as a measurable trait does have a research literature, and the lived experience described by RSD is consistent and real. Use the word if it helps you; do not let anyone hand it to you as a diagnosis, and do not use it on yourself as one.
:::

## Why this travels with ADHD

Two reasons, and both are worth knowing because they are not about character.

**Regulation.** The spike is the same alarm everyone has; what differs is how fast it is brought back down. Where emotional regulation is less reliable, the reaction goes higher and stays longer — so the same cue produces an afternoon rather than a minute.

**Evidence.** If you grew up being told to try harder, sit still, stop talking, apply yourself — thousands of small corrections, many of them delivered as disappointment — then disapproval is not an abstract fear. It is the most heavily reinforced social pattern of your childhood. Your system is not malfunctioning. It is well trained.

## What the spike does to your judgement

For about twenty minutes, specific distortions run:

- **Mind reading.** You fill in their thoughts and treat the guess as information.
- **The whole relationship, re-scored.** One flat message re-rates the friendship, the job, the marriage.
- **Urgency.** The pressure to fix it *right now* — the apology, the over-explanation, the three-paragraph message — is a symptom of the spike, not a feature of the situation.
- **Pre-emptive withdrawal.** The opposite move: go cold first, cancel, be unavailable. Less visible from outside, equally driven.

Everything you do in those twenty minutes, you will reassess later. That is the single most useful fact on this page.

## What actually helps

### 1. Buy twenty minutes

The spike peaks and drops. Not gone — down to a size you can think around.

So the rule is simple and absolute: **no replies, no apologies, no cancelling, no bold moves for twenty minutes.** Write the message if you need to. Do not send it. Almost every regrettable move in this pattern happens inside that window.

### 2. Name it as the pattern, not as information

Out loud if you can: *"This is the spike. It is not new information about me."*

That is not positive thinking. It is accurate categorisation — you are labelling an internal event rather than accepting a verdict, and labelling reliably lowers the temperature.

### 3. Separate what was said from what you added

Two lines on paper:
- **Said:** "thanks, got it."
- **Added:** "I'm being pushed out."

Seeing them stacked usually ends it, because the gap is visible and absurd in a way it never is inside your head.

### 4. Ask, plainly, after the window

If it still matters in the morning, one clean sentence: *"Was that okay? I couldn't read your tone."*

Short, no preamble, no apology. Most of the time you get an answer in six words and it has nothing to do with you.

### 5. Keep the evidence file

Write down compliments verbatim, with who said it. You will not want it on good days. You will want it at 11pm, when your brain reports that nobody has ever been glad to have you around — and a file in specific language beats a feeling in a way reassurance cannot.

::: try The next flat message
Do nothing for twenty minutes. Write the two lines — what was said, what you added. Then decide whether there is anything to ask. That sequence, repeated, is how the gap between the signal and the spiral gets shorter.
:::

## When to get help with it

If this costs you jobs, friendships or relationships, if you organise your life around never being disapproved of, or if the crash comes with hopelessness that lasts days — that is worth taking to a professional who understands ADHD.

Not because you are broken. Because this particular pattern responds much better to proper support than to trying harder on your own, and "trying harder" is probably the exact instruction that trained it in the first place.
